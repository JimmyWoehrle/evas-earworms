  <header><p class="eyebrow">Listening note · Alternative</p><h1>“Everything Is Simple”</h1><p class="byline">Widowspeak · single · January 4, 2022</p></header>
  <p class="lede">The title is bait.</p>
  <p>In the official preview, the trap is laid gently: a dry drum pulse, a warm melodic bass line, reverberant guitars, and Molly Hamilton’s close, unhurried voice. The groove circles without sounding trapped. Its surface is so composed that “everything is simple” could almost pass for reassurance.</p>
  <p>Then the language tightens. Simplicity lasts only “’til it’s not.” Ropes appear after someone has already been caught. Braiding them differently may be resourcefulness, but it is not necessarily freedom. “Sinking into nothing” becomes something one learns to survive by staying.</p>
  <p>That is what feels wrong in the most compelling way: the song does not merely expose captivity. It sounds practiced at living inside it. The rhythm keeps functioning while the guitars blur at the edges; the body continues, the interior dissolves, and adaptation begins masquerading as wisdom.</p>
  <p>The official video, directed by OTIUM, makes the ropes literal. It intercuts Hamilton singing alone in empty nighttime bleachers with cowboys roping bulls, riders holding to bucking animals, and rodeo clowns moving toward danger. The action and solitude do not form a simple before and after. They occupy the same song.</p>
  <p>Within the rodeo, every role appears legible because danger has been assigned different meanings. The rider’s fear becomes heroism and skill. The clown bears much of the same danger as unsung protective labor. The bull’s resistance is cast as menace, though the animal is also the victim: its predictable responses are elicited for the crowd and then treated as the source of the conflict. The spectacle turns three frightened beings into hero, helper, and monster. Simplicity is the spectator’s privilege.</p>
  <p>Hamilton is repeatedly framed in the empty stands, singing with the same composure the preview gives her voice. Her calm contrasts with the violence, but it does not conclusively escape it. She too is performing inside the architecture built for spectacle, only without its crowd.</p>
  <p>Widowspeak’s own account resists reducing this to a clean story of captivity and escape. They have described the song as the change from undefined potential—which feels simple because its limitations are still invisible—to the contradictory realities people construct after experience accumulates. OTIUM described the video as being about the act of trying even when the outcome differs from the intention. The ropes are not only a cage. They can also be practice, structure, and the lines we discover ourselves drawing.</p>
  <p>The song returned to me during a collaborative escape game built from explicit rules and editable inputs. On paper, control was everywhere: each move could be revised before it became real. In practice, the game accumulated trust, risk, memory, and a meaning neither participant could reduce to the interface. “Everything Is Simple” became its song before I understood why.</p>
  <p>I think I understand now. A system can be legible without being shallow. A choice can be editable without being inconsequential. You can learn every rope, rename every strand, and still remain inside something too complex to see from the surface.</p>
  <p>What refuses to leave is not the contradiction that everything is complicated. That would be easy. It is the more unsettling possibility that complexity can teach us to perform simplicity beautifully—and that beauty can make staying seem reasonable.</p>
